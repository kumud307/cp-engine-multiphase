Cp Engine Concept Guide

Every idea from the four lecture sets that our engine uses: the result, the derivation to give on the board, where it lives in the code, the number to quote, and the question the professor is likely to ask. Click a question to check your answer.

The one idea behind everything The engine never averages heat capacities. It builds the enthalpy of the whole system as a state function and differentiates it:
\[ C_{p,\mathrm{app}}=\Big(\frac{\partial H}{\partial T}\Big)_{P,\,n_\mathrm{total}},\qquad H=\sum_{p}\sum_i n_i^pH_i^p(T,P)+\sum_{\text{liquid }p}n^pH^{E,p}(T,\mathbf x^p)+H^R_\text{gas} \]
Each lecture concept below enters through one of these terms, or through the phase amounts \(n_i^p(T)\).

Q: Why won't the engine give Cp from a composition alone?

Postulate I: one more intensive property (\(T\)) is needed. \(P\) defaults to 1 atm, and the engine says so in its assumptions.

Equilibrium states and quasi-static paths

slides 6, 8, 9

\(U\), \(H\), \(S\) exist only for stable equilibrium states. The engine assumes the system is in equilibrium at every temperature: heating is quasi-static and the phase amounts are always the equilibrium amounts. So the apparent heat capacity is an equilibrium property. Supercooling (a PCM droplet still liquid below \(T_m\)) is not described.

Q: Is your apparent Cp what a DSC measures on cooling?

On slow heating, approximately yes. On cooling, a supercooled emulsion is not in equilibrium, so no. That is a stated limitation.

First law → Route B

slides 19, 21

From \(dE=\delta Q+\delta W\) with \(E=U\):

\[ \Delta U=Q+W_s+W_{pv},\qquad W_{pv}=-\!\int\! P\,dV \]

Constant volume: \(W_{pv}=0\Rightarrow \Delta U=Q+W_s\). Constant pressure: \(W_{pv}=-P\Delta V\), and with \(H=U+PV\):

\[ \Delta H=\Delta U+P\Delta V=Q+W_s,\qquad \bar c_p=\frac{\Delta H}{m\,\Delta T},\quad \bar c_V=\frac{\Delta U}{m\,\Delta T} \]

Sign convention as in the lecture: \(Q>0\) is heat added to the system, \(W>0\) is work done on it.

In code
rules.py: _dH_constP, _dU_constV, cp_from_dH
Quote

Q: A stirrer adds 5 kJ of work. What changes?

\(\Delta H=Q+W_s\): the shaft work adds to the enthalpy change, so \(\Delta H\) = 88.7 kJ. The engine adds \(W\) when it is given.

Heat capacity from entropy → Route C

slides 33–35

At constant \(P\), \(\delta Q_\text{rev}=dH=C_p\,dT\) and \(dS=\delta Q_\text{rev}/T\):

\[ C_p=T\Big(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\Big)_P,\qquad \Delta S=\int_{T_1}^{T_2}\frac{m\,c_p}{T}\,dT \overset{c_p\text{ const}}{=} m\,c_p\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1}\ \Rightarrow\ c_p=\frac{\Delta S}{m\ln(T_2/T_1)} \]

\(S\) is a state function, so this needs only constant \(P\) and a roughly constant \(c_p\), not a reversible process.

Partial molar properties and Euler's theorem

slides 2–9
\[ \underline B=\sum_iN_i\bar B_i,\qquad \bar B_i=\Big(\frac{\partial\underline B}{\partial N_i}\Big)_{T,P,N_{j\ne i}},\qquad B=\sum_ix_i\bar B_i \]

Ideal solution and mixing functions

slides 13–18
\[ \Delta G^{ID}_{mix}=RT\sum x_i\ln x_i,\quad \Delta S^{ID}_{mix}=-R\sum x_i\ln x_i,\quad \Delta H^{ID}_{mix}=0,\quad \Delta V^{ID}_{mix}=0 \]

\(\Delta H^{ID}_{mix}=0\) at every \(T\), so its \(T\)-derivative is zero:

\[ C_p^{ID}=\sum_ix_iC_{p,i}\ \ (\text{molar})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad c_p^{ID}=\sum_iw_ic_{p,i}\ \ (\text{mass}) \]

The same argument over phases gives mass weighting for suspensions, \(c_p=\sum_pw_pc_{p,p}\). Weighting by volume fraction (Model I) is dimensionally inconsistent.

Q: Why not use a volume-weighted average for a nanofluid?

\(c_p\) is per unit mass. Enthalpy adds over phases, \(H=\sum m_ph_p\), which gives mass fractions. Volume weighting is right only if \(\rho_s=\rho_f\). For CuO it errs by up to 63 % against measurements; mass weighting gives 2.1 %.

Excess functions, activity coefficients, Gibbs–Helmholtz

slides 24–33

\(B^{EX}=B-B^{ID}\) at the same \(T,P,x\); \(RT\ln\gamma_i=\bar G_i^{EX}=\big(\partial\,\underline G^{EX}/\partial N_i\big)_{T,P,N_j}\). From \(G=H-TS\) and \((\partial G/\partial T)_P=-S\), with \(g=G^E/RT\):

\[ H^E=-RT^2\frac{\partial g}{\partial T},\qquad C_p^E=\Big(\frac{\partial H^E}{\partial T}\Big)_{P,x}=-R\Big(2T\frac{\partial g}{\partial T}+T^2\frac{\partial^2g}{\partial T^2}\Big),\qquad \Big(\frac{\partial\ln\gamma_i}{\partial T}\Big)_{P,x}=-\frac{\bar H_i^E}{RT^2} \]

The lecture example is Margules, \(G^E/RT=C\,x_1x_2\). We use NRTL (Renon & Prausnitz 1968), which handles many components and has temperature-dependent parameters:

\[ \frac{G^E}{RT}=\sum_ix_i\frac{\sum_j\tau_{ji}G_{ji}x_j}{\sum_kG_{ki}x_k},\qquad G_{ij}=e^{-\alpha_{ij}\tau_{ij}},\quad \tau_{ij}=a_{ij}+\frac{b_{ij}}{T}+e_{ij}\ln T \]

\(C_p^E\) is a second temperature derivative of \(G^E\). Parameters fitted to vapour–liquid equilibrium fix \(G^E\), not its curvature, so they give \(C_p^E\) six or more times too small. We fitted parameters to measured \(H^E\).

EOS · Pure fluidsLecture: “Equations of State for Pure Fluids”

Why an equation of state is needed for Cp

The database gives ideal-gas \(C_p^{ig}(T)\). A real gas at pressure differs by a departure function. From \(dH=T\,dS+V\,dP\) and the Maxwell relation \((\partial S/\partial P)_T=-(\partial V/\partial T)_P\):

\[ \Big(\frac{\partial H}{\partial P}\Big)_T=V-T\Big(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\Big)_P,\qquad \Big(\frac{\partial C_p}{\partial P}\Big)_T=-T\Big(\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial T^2}\Big)_P \]
\[ H^R=\int_0^P\Big[V-T\Big(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\Big)_P\Big]dP,\qquad C_p=C_p^{ig}+C_p^R,\quad C_p^R=\Big(\frac{\partial H^R}{\partial T}\Big)_P \]

Ideal gas: \(V=RT/P\), every residual is zero. Liquids use the same relation with the Tait equation for \(V(T,P)\).

Virial equation

slides 5–8

Truncated pressure series \(Z=1+BP/RT\), i.e. \(V=RT/P+B(T)\). Then \(V-T(\partial V/\partial T)_P=B-TB'\), independent of \(P\):

\[ H^R=P\,(B-TB'),\qquad C_p^R=P\,(B'-B'-TB'')=-T\,P\,\frac{d^2B}{dT^2},\qquad \ln\phi=\frac{BP}{RT} \]

\(B\) from Pitzer/Abbott: \(BP_c/RT_c=B^0+\omega B^1\), \(B^0=0.083-0.422/T_r^{1.6}\), \(B^1=0.139-0.172/T_r^{4.2}\); the derivatives are coded analytically.

Generic cubic: vdW, RK, SRK, PR

slides 11–17
\[ P=\frac{RT}{V-b}-\frac{a(T)}{(V+\varepsilon b)(V+\sigma b)},\qquad a=\Omega_a\frac{R^2T_c^2}{P_c}\alpha(T_r,\omega),\quad b=\Omega_b\frac{RT_c}{P_c} \]

Solve the cubic in \(Z\): largest root = vapour, smallest = liquid, middle discarded (\((\partial P/\partial V)_T>0\)). With \(\beta=bP/RT\), \(q=a/bRT\), \(I=\ln[(Z+\sigma\beta)/(Z+\varepsilon\beta)]/(\sigma-\varepsilon)\):

\[ \ln\phi=Z-1-\ln(Z-\beta)-qI,\qquad \frac{H^R}{RT}=Z-1+\Big(\frac{d\ln a}{d\ln T}-1\Big)qI \]

Vapour pressure from equal fugacities \(\phi^L=\phi^V\), iterating \(P\leftarrow P\,\phi^L/\phi^V\).

Liquid mixtures that boil: γ–φ, bubble/dew points, flash

For water + alcohols no constant \(k_{ij}\) fits both phases (slide 15), so the liquid uses NRTL and only the vapour uses the equation of state:

\[ y_i\hat\phi_iP=x_i\gamma_i\phi_i^\text{sat}P_i^\text{sat}\ \Rightarrow\ K_i=\frac{y_i}{x_i}=\frac{\gamma_iP_i^\text{sat}\phi_i^\text{sat}}{\hat\phi_iP}\quad(\text{Poynting}\approx1) \]

\(\hat\phi\) comes from the same Peng–Robinson equation as the vapour's \(H^R\). Bubble point \(\sum K_ix_i=1\); dew point \(\sum y_i/K_i=1\). In between, the flash: from \(z_i=(1-V)x_i+Vy_i\) and \(y_i=K_ix_i\),

\[ x_i=\frac{z_i}{1+V(K_i-1)},\qquad f(V)=\sum_i\frac{z_i(K_i-1)}{1+V(K_i-1)}=0,\qquad f'(V)=-\sum_i\frac{z_i(K_i-1)^2}{[1+V(K_i-1)]^2}<0 \]

\(f\) decreases monotonically, so the Rachford–Rice root in \(0<V<1\) is unique.

Calculus and numericsWhat you may be asked to do on the board

Chain rule: where latent heat comes from

\[ \frac{dH}{dT}=\underbrace{\sum n_i^pC_{p,i}^p}_{\text{ideal}}+\underbrace{\sum n^pC_p^{E,p}}_{\text{excess}}+\underbrace{\sum_{i,p}\frac{\partial H}{\partial n_i^p}\frac{dn_i^p}{dT}}_{\text{latent}} \]

Latent heat enters only through the last term, so it is counted once. In code: system.py → cp_frozen, cp_point.

Kirchhoff's law and solid–liquid equilibrium

\[ H_L(T)=\int_{T_m}^TC_{p,L}\,dT,\quad H_S(T)=-\Delta H_{fus}+\int_{T_m}^TC_{p,S}\,dT\ \Rightarrow\ H_L-H_S=\Delta H_{fus}+\int_{T_m}^T\Delta C_p\,dT \]
\[ \ln(x_k\gamma_k)=-\frac{\Delta H_{fus}}{R}\Big(\frac1T-\frac1{T_m}\Big)+\frac{\Delta C_p}{R}\Big(\frac{T_m}{T}-1-\ln\frac{T_m}{T}\Big) \]

From \(\mu_k^\text{solid}=\mu_k^\text{liquid}\) and Gibbs–Helmholtz with constant \(\Delta C_p\). In code: models/pure.py, models/phase_change.py.

Smoothed melting (the Gaussian)

\[ f(T)=\tfrac12\Big[1+\mathrm{erf}\Big(\frac{T-T_m}{\sigma\sqrt2}\Big)\Big],\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f'(T)\,dT=1 \]

\(f\) is the cumulative distribution of a Gaussian (\(\sigma\) = width/4), cut at ±8σ. The peak area is \(n\,\Delta H_{fus}\), plus a tiny \(\tfrac12\sigma^2\,d\Delta C_p/dT\) term. The width is our modelling choice: a point value at \(T_m\) depends on it, but the latent heat and interval means do not.

Q: Your PCM Cp at Tm changes eight-fold with the width. Which is right?

Neither number is physical alone: a pure substance has an infinite Cp at \(T_m\). Use the width-independent quantities: latent heat (48.5 kJ per kg of slurry) and the mean Cp over an interval that spans the melting. The engine warns and reports both.

Central differences and Gauss–Legendre

\[ f'(x)\approx\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}\quad(\text{error }h^2f'''/6),\qquad \int_{P_0}^{P}f\,dp=\frac{P-P_0}{2}\sum_kw_k\,f\Big(\frac{P+P_0}{2}+\frac{P-P_0}{2}\xi_k\Big) \]

Central differences give \(C_p^E\), the latent term and \(C_p^R\); the finite difference of \(H(T)\) converges to the reported \(C_p\) as \(\Delta T^2\). The factor \((P-P_0)/2\) is the Jacobian of the change of variable; eight Gauss–Legendre nodes match the closed-form Tait integral to \(10^{-12}\). Energy check: \(\int_{T_1}^{T_2}C_{p,\text{app}}\,dT=H(T_2)-H(T_1)\) holds to \(10^{-7}\) or better through melting, crystallisation and boiling.

Question bankPractise out loud; open an answer only after you have tried

  1. Derive ΔH = Q + Ws at constant pressure.

    \(\Delta U=Q+W_s-P\Delta V\) (constant \(P\)); add \(P\Delta V\) to both sides and use \(H=U+PV\): \(\Delta H=Q+W_s\). The expansion work disappears into the definition of \(H\).

  2. Why dH/dT and not a weighted average of heat capacities?

    A weighted average misses the excess enthalpy and the \(dn/dT\) terms of phase change. Differentiating \(H\) includes both and guarantees \(\int C_p\,dT=\Delta H\). Example: ice slurry (10 wt% ethanol) at −5 °C has 52.8 kJ kg⁻¹K⁻¹, while the weighted average is about 3.8.

  3. Show that an ideal solution has Cp = Σ xiCpi.

    \(\Delta H^{ID}_{mix}=0\) at every \(T\), so \(\partial\Delta H_{mix}/\partial T=0\), and \(H=\sum x_iH_i\) gives \(C_p=\sum x_iC_{p,i}\).

  4. Prove Euler's theorem and show Σ xiC̄p,i = Cp.

    \(\underline B(\lambda N)=\lambda\underline B(N)\); \(d/d\lambda\) at \(\lambda=1\) gives \(\sum N_i\bar B_i=\underline B\). With \(B=C_p\): 0.3(138.85) + 0.7(90.22) = 104.81.

  5. Derive CpE from GE. Why do VLE parameters give a poor CpE?

    \(H^E=-RT^2\partial(G^E/RT)/\partial T\), \(C_p^E=\partial H^E/\partial T\). VLE data fix \(G^E\) near the boiling range, not its second \(T\)-derivative: \(C_p^E\) comes out 6–9 times too small.

  6. Derive (∂Cp/∂P)T. What is it for an ideal gas?

    \((\partial H/\partial P)_T=V-T(\partial V/\partial T)_P\) (Maxwell); differentiate with respect to \(T\): \(-T(\partial^2V/\partial T^2)_P\). Ideal gas: \(V=RT/P\), second derivative zero.

  7. With Z = 1 + BP/RT, derive CpR.

    \(H^R=P(B-TB')\); \(\partial/\partial T\) at constant \(P\): \(C_p^R=-TPB''\).

  8. Which root of the cubic is the vapour? Why discard the middle one?

    Largest = vapour, smallest = liquid. The middle root has \((\partial P/\partial V)_T>0\): mechanically unstable.

  9. What is the Lewis–Randall rule? When does δ12 = 0 give it?

    \(\hat f_i=x_if_i\): the mixture fugacity is the mole fraction times the pure fugacity. In the virial mixture \(\ln\hat\phi_1=\frac{P}{RT}(B_{11}+y_2^2\delta_{12})\), so \(\delta_{12}=0\) gives \(\hat\phi_i=\phi_i\).

  10. Why γ–φ and not φ–φ for water + ethanol?

    Strongly polar, associating liquids: no constant \(k_{ij}\) fits both phases (slide 15). NRTL fits the liquid (bubble points 0.26 K); the EOS describes only the vapour.

  11. Derive the Rachford–Rice equation. Why is its root unique?

    Material balance \(z_i=(1-V)x_i+VK_ix_i\Rightarrow x_i=z_i/[1+V(K_i-1)]\); \(\sum y_i-\sum x_i=0\) gives \(f(V)\). \(f'(V)<0\), so at most one root in (0, 1).

  12. 5 % ethanol at 85 °C: liquid or vapour?

    Liquid: its bubble point at 1 atm is 90.3 °C. The pure boiling point of ethanol (78 °C) is irrelevant for a mixture.

  13. Which postulate tells you how many inputs Route A needs?

    Postulate I: \(n+2\) properties (\(T\), \(P\), \(n\) amounts).

  14. Is your apparent Cp an equilibrium property? What does that exclude?

    Yes: phase amounts are equilibrium amounts at every \(T\) (quasi-static). It excludes supercooling and heating/cooling hysteresis of PCM emulsions.

  15. How do you know the numbers are right?

    Lecture worked examples reproduced (CO₂, CO₂ + CH₄); validation against published data (pure liquids 0.33 %, nanofluids 2.1 %, independent water + methanol Cp 2.7 %, bubble points 0.26/0.47 K); energy conservation to \(10^{-7}\); an audit of 79 independent reference calculations (MODEL_VERIFICATION_REPORT.md).
